Hybrid Cloud Mesh

Role + Timeline

UX Designer + 2 years

Objective

The goal is to make connecting and managing applications across hybrid and multi-cloud environments simple, secure, and efficient—helping businesses improve performance, maintain control, and scale their cloud operations with less hassle.

Overview

Traditional networking approaches are complex, slow to deploy, and lack visibility, making it difficult for teams to manage performance, enforce security, and scale operations. There’s a need for a simpler, automated, app-centric connectivity solution. IBM Hybrid Cloud Mesh simplifies secure, app-centric connectivity across hybrid and multi-cloud environments. It uses automated policies, zero-trust security, and centralized management to speed up deployment, reduce complexity, and improve performance—enabling teams to connect, manage, and scale workloads efficiently while maintaining visibility, control, and resilience. As a designer and hands-on contributor, I help shape the overall UX direction while working closely with product and engineering teams. I lead design on key features, dive into user research, and create clear, easy workflows that tackle the tricky hybrid and multi-cloud networking challenges around security and scaling.

Problem statement

How can organizations securely and efficiently connect applications across hybrid and multi-cloud environments while reducing complexity, improving visibility, and enabling faster deployment and scalability?

Design process

I kicked off the design process by talking to users and reviewing early feedback to understand their needs, frustrations, and how they were currently navigating hybrid and multi-cloud connectivity. These conversations helped surface real pain points—things like complex setup processes, limited visibility, and the lack of flexibility when managing connections across environments. From there, I partnered closely with product managers and engineers to gather requirements and make sure our design ideas aligned with technical constraints and business priorities.
To get a clearer picture of the full experience, I mapped out user journeys that reflected different use cases and scenarios. This helped identify where users were getting stuck, where expectations weren’t being met, and where we had opportunities to streamline the process. With that foundation, I created low-fidelity prototypes to explore different concepts quickly. These early sketches made it easier to share ideas, get feedback from users and stakeholders, and refine the experience before investing in high-fidelity design.

Throughout the process, I focused on staying user-centered while keeping technical realities in mind. The goal was to design something that not only solved key user problems but also fit naturally into the product’s architecture and roadmap.

Competitive analysis

As part of the research phase, we conducted a comparative analysis to understand how users currently manage multiple deployments across hybrid cloud environments. This involved reviewing a mix of tools commonly used for cloud networking, infrastructure management, and connectivity—ranging from cloud-native solutions to third-party platforms like OpenShift. Through user interviews and surveys, it became clear that while many teams piece together a combination of tools depending on their cloud provider and architecture, Red Hat emerged as the most widely used and trusted, especially in enterprise environments. Users frequently cited Red Hat as go-to solutions for managing deployments, orchestrating workloads, and enforcing policy across both private and public cloud infrastructure. This discovery helped us shape our design strategy by highlighting the importance of interoperability, open standards, and integrations. It also emphasized the need for a user experience that complements existing Red Hat workflows, rather than competing with them. By aligning our designs with familiar mental models and patterns from Red Hat tools, we aimed to reduce the learning curve and build trust with users already invested in that ecosystem.

Final deliverables

Designing the final deliverable for the initial general release of Mesh was a collaborative effort focused on creating a user-friendly, powerful interface for both infrastructure and application operations teams. Early on, I worked with stakeholders to align on our two core user groups: platform engineers managing cloud environments, and developers responsible for deploying and connecting workloads. Each group had distinct responsibilities and pain points, which directly informed how we prioritized features and structured the user experience. We gathered requirements by speaking with internal teams and early users to understand current practices for managing multi-cloud connectivity—especially from customers with existing Red Hat gateway deployments. Supporting these “brownfield” environments meant designing a UI that could detect, import, and integrate existing infrastructure without disruption. A key part of the design was the onboarding flow, which guided users through connecting their cloud accounts, setting up network gateways, and registering applications and services. We focused on making this process approachable while maintaining the flexibility needed for more complex setups. Another central feature was the topology view—a visual map of all connected resources across environments. This gave users a high-level overview of their network and the ability to drill down into traffic metrics and performance insights, helping them monitor health, troubleshoot issues, and optimize connectivity.

Challenges and results

One of the major challenges we faced in the design process was the pivot from using Skupper gateways to adopting Red Hat Service Interconnect (RHSI) gateways. While Skupper had been our initial solution for enabling secure communication across hybrid cloud environments, it became clear that it posed limitations in terms of scalability, ease of use, and alignment with enterprise tooling.
Through user research and customer feedback, we discovered that many of our existing users struggled with Skupper’s operational complexity and unfamiliar setup flows. At the same time, we noticed growing interest from potential customers already using Red Hat tooling for their cloud infrastructure management. These organizations were looking for solutions that fit more seamlessly into their existing environments, with better support and stronger enterprise backing.
Transitioning to Red Hat Service Interconnect was not without friction—it required rethinking core parts of the user journey, adjusting technical assumptions, and redesigning connection flows. However, this pivot allowed us to better support current customers with more stable, enterprise-grade capabilities while also opening doors to new adoption by integrating with platforms users already trust.
The design challenge became creating a smoother, more familiar experience that reflected RHSI’s strengths while minimizing disruption for existing users. Meeting tight deadlines for customer demos while managing workloads in fast-paced monthly release cycles came with several technical and design challenges. One of the biggest hurdles was aligning design timelines with engineering sprints—especially when we were working on high-impact features that needed to be demo-ready before they were fully production-ready.
With limited time and evolving requirements, we often had to design and iterate quickly, sometimes without having all the backend functionality in place. This meant closely collaborating with engineers to understand what could realistically be demoed in each cycle, and prioritizing design efforts to focus on what would deliver the most customer value with the least risk. In some cases, we created lightweight, front-end-only prototypes or demo-specific interactions to showcase a feature’s potential without waiting for the full backend integration.
At the same time, managing workloads meant juggling between short-term needs (like demo polish and bug fixes) and long-term feature development. To stay on track, I worked closely with PMs to plan design work ahead of engineering, maintained clear documentation, and made use of design ops practices to streamline feedback and handoff. This balance helped us deliver impactful demos and stay on course through fast-moving, monthly release cycles.
Switching to Red Hat Service Interconnect (RHSI) gateways made a big difference for our users and the overall product experience. Many of our customers were already using Red Hat tools like OpenShift, so this change let them plug into something familiar without adding new complexity. It gave them a smoother, more reliable way to connect workloads across hybrid and multi-cloud environments—something that used to be a lot trickier and more manual. RHSI made setup easier, cut down on confusion, and reduced the time it took to get up and running. Because it fits naturally into existing
Red Hat environments, teams didn’t have to reinvent their workflows or take on extra tools to manage. That familiarity helped build trust and made adoption feel more straightforward. It also meant fewer support issues and more consistent performance across deployments, especially when it came to scaling or managing complex environments. In the end, this move helped us support existing users more effectively and made the product more appealing to new customers already in the Red Hat ecosystem. It’s been a key step in making our hybrid cloud networking more user-friendly and enterprise-ready.
